# Chapter 094 — Seeds of the Black Lotus

*[[Сурадж Ка Гхар|Suraj Ka Ghar]], 106 NE, summer*

While [[Томас Робинсон|Tom]], [[Урист Китин|Urist]] and [[Мэй Ву|Mei]] stood in the alley over three dead agents of the [[Чёрный Лотос|Black Lotus]] and a fourth who had cast himself into unwaking sleep, [[Гилья Кангарлавехотри|Gilja]] was finishing his conversation at the city hospital.

[[Шубханкари|Shubhankari]] had left him two paths. He could take [[Самир Тхакур|Samir's]] blood and vital strength with a raktapatra and give them to the goddess, or persuade the Raksha to present himself willingly at the Blood Forge with the others when the time came. Samir was still obeying Gilja and had made no attempt to flee, so the choice could be postponed for a little while. Before Gilja left, the rescued [[Савитри Эклунд|Savitri]] had managed to explain that the meeting with [[Чори Марджари|Chori]] was to take place near the laundry. He went in search of his friends while night still held Vanpur.

Dawn had yet to venture into the narrow passage. Three black-clad bodies lay between the close walls. There had been no rain, yet the stones beneath them shone with blood. The fourth man breathed deeply and evenly—far too peacefully for someone who had just driven a black claw into his own neck. The first stroke of a temple gong sounded in the distance, answered by the whistle of the city watch.

The laundry lines trembled above the alley. A short young woman in a travelling cloak sat upon the edge of a roof, one hand gripping a drainpipe while the other turned one of [[Мерката|Mercate's]] feathers between her fingers.

Chori warned that anyone who stayed would have to explain the three dead men to the watch. Those who followed her, however, might learn something about the fourth. She had no intention of repeating the invitation.

She leapt lightly to the next roof, crossed to the far corner and stopped, nevertheless giving them a chance to follow.

Gilja entered the alley from the direction of the hospital. He had no intention of skulking through his own city and took Chori's remark about the watch almost as a personal slight.

Chori called down that he ought to get his friends away before the watch rounded up the lot of them; he could prove his right to command the city afterwards.

There was no time to argue. The whistles were drawing closer, and a claim of self-defence would sound unconvincing from armed foreigners found standing over the dead.

The travellers did not summon the watch. Instead, they turned the alley into the scene of an internal Black Lotus execution. They had already removed the rings and clawed gloves from the dead. Now they altered the wounds with their own blades, drew lotuses upon the sand and walls, and arranged the bodies as though the accomplices had killed one another during a quarrel. They killed the sleeping man as well: a living witness might undo their whole invention, even if no one knew whether he could ever be woken.

Mei tore her expensive Lang-An silk, preparing to play a robbed traveller should the need arise. They left a Rajati curse about rats and informers upon the wall, complete with one misspelt letter that lent the foreign threat an almost convincing carelessness. When the watch entered the alley, they were meant to see not the trail of four Imitei but someone else's war, begun long before the travellers arrived.

Chori did not wait beside the bodies. She went ahead and led them into a dyers' courtyard near the laundry. It was about half past four in the morning. Long lengths of cloth the colour of a deep evening sky hung between the flat roofs; stray blue drops fell from them, leaving coin-sized shadows upon the stone. Two women silently lit the furnaces while a boy rolled a barrel of water towards them. They scarcely looked at the strangers.

Chori called to the heroes before they noticed her. The girl had settled upon a stone crossbeam above an old dyeing vat, with a ledge no wider than a hand beneath her bare heel. She tossed the third feather to Tom, assured him that she had stolen nothing else from him and proposed that they finally discuss the matter at hand.

Chori had three conditions. No one would draw a weapon while they spoke. No one would ask the names of the laundresses sheltering her. And if she told them everything she knew about the Lotus, the heroes would help her discover how to wake the sleepers.

Gilja objected that even the gods did not know how to wake the sleepers and that almost no time remained for the search. Chori stubbornly reminded him that her own time was running out as well, yet she had no intention of abandoning the search.

Her father, [[Шанкар Марджари|Shankar Marjari]], was dying of [[Ракта-Доша|Rakta-Dosha]], growing weaker with every day. In Vanpur, Chori heard that an enormous sum could buy a brief reprieve from death. Her family had no such money, so the girl pretended that she wished to work for the traders in black sleep. Once she obtained one of their blades, she stole it, returned to [[Деревня Бадракали|Badrakali]] and put not only her father but several other patients already at death's threshold to sleep.

Chori did not claim to have saved them. She had merely stopped the disease from killing them before a true treatment appeared. Yet if that stolen time was to mean anything, she now had to find a way to bring them out of the sleep.

Looking not at the heroes but into the blue water of the vat, Chori admitted that she had expected to stop the disease first and deal with the consequences later. She now needed no lecture from anyone to understand how dreadful that order of choices had proved.

The black sleep reminded Chori of the powers of the nagas in the eastern marshes. She had stolen Mercate's feathers because she heard that the nagas collected them for some purpose, which meant there might be room for a bargain. Outside the city, the girl had indeed met [[Шаша|Shasha]].

Chori described Shasha as surprisingly sweet and even compared her to a fluffy little rabbit. The moment she noticed the travellers' expressions, the girl frowned and hastened to clarify that she had not given the naga the feathers.

Chori had also seen the second Raksha whom Gilja sought. He lived at the temple in Vanpur, and the monks had no intention of betraying him. At times, an ancient grey-bearded man in a hood appeared at his side. The two could stand in silence for a long while before going their separate ways. Samir knew nothing of this; for the past few weeks, he had scarcely left the hospital wards.

Chori could say more about the people of the Black Lotus. One of them was the husband of a woman who sold jewellery at the market. He seldom left his house, but attended meetings and knew the western quarters of Vanpur perhaps even better than Chori did. She had tried to follow him several times and lost the trail every time. [[Вирадж Сомани|Viraj Somani]], whom the heroes had taken for a Lotus contact, was in her judgement merely a swindler and had not been seen for two days. Another market intermediary might now be lying among the dead in the alley. The owner of one warehouse had once transported goods for the Lotus but had since been expelled from their circle. The only man who might have known the truth about the obsidian had been the sleeping attacker—the very man the travellers had just killed.

After speaking with Mercate, the heroes had believed that ordinary obsidian was somehow imbued with the power of eternal sleep. Chori could neither confirm nor disprove the idea. Shipments of stone, black shards and strange marks still linked Vanpur to [[Город Сангалла|Sangalla]], but the nearest available thread led not to mines or jungles but to a jeweller's closed door.

On Tuesday, after the recent delivery from Lang-An, the jeweller's wife was due to display new wares. Chori hoped the husband would emerge to inspect them; if he did not, she would watch from the rooftops to see whether he left the house by another way.

They wrapped Gilja in cloth to conceal the marks of a Raksha. Urist was to play a wealthy patron who wanted his family plant—a cactus—rendered in gold. Mei took the part of his slave from Lang-An. Tom and Chori climbed to the roofs, from where they could watch both the shop and the surrounding streets.

By the start of trade, the market already roared with voices. Guards still investigating the night's massacre stopped the travellers and asked several questions, but found no reason to hold them. Urist replied that they had nothing to do with the affair and led his companions onwards with the air of a man for whom the city watch was merely an impediment to spending money.

The jeweller never emerged from the house. His wife was busy behind the stall. Urist addressed her as a customer accustomed to bows: he examined the rings critically, demanded a piece made to a particular design and mentioned his family emblem. Mei stood beside him with downcast eyes, pretending to be someone else's property. The merchant swiftly decided that a man of means stood before her. Leaning towards the doorway, she whispered to her husband that the very head of Ulehey appeared to have visited them.

To make the deception more convincing, Urist began to humiliate his ‘slave’ loudly and imperiously. Mei endured it for as long as she could. Then her feelings escaped with the flames.

The linen cloth beneath the displayed rings caught fire. Flames passed to the wooden table, licked the awning and seized the clothes of those beside the stall. Urist grabbed Mei, dragged her to the water trough and plunged her in, continuing to curse as though he were punishing a wilful servant rather than saving his friend.

Inside the house, the shopkeeper's clothes ignited. The burning man ran into the street and made for the well. Tom dropped from the roof, caught up a supply of water and helped smother the flames before the burns became fatal.

The rescued man was [[Джевахар Ашма|Djevahar Ashma]]. As soon as he recovered his senses, he fell to his knees before Tom and promised him jewellery, a favourable contract and anything else the shop could offer, if only the fire were stopped before it ruined the family.

Tom asked only for a conversation without witnesses. Urist squeezed into the house behind them before the jeweller's wife could block the door. The moment the bolt slid home, Djevahar's gratitude turned to silence.

Threats achieved what the fire had not. Djevahar saw before him people who knew of his connection to the Lotus, while his wife remained beyond the thin wall. He answered in haste, afraid to pause over the wrong word. The heroes watched the jeweller's face and understood that, for the moment at least, fear left him no strength for invention.

The Black Lotus sold neither needles nor ornaments, but injections. For a price, they cast the sick into sleep and called it a reprieve from death by Rakta-Dosha. They left families with hope, then demanded for that hope everything the families still had to give.

The Lotus, however, had not begun with the disease. Its people had once been poachers who killed elephants in the jungle. Within some of the dead animals, they found not ordinary blood vessels but black, vitrified veins. The needles were neither cut from obsidian nor imbued with some foreign power—they were found ready-made inside the dead elephants. Ordinary volcanic glass merely resembled this strange substance and had therefore led the heroes to a false conclusion. Djevahar did not know who was bringing entire shipments of true obsidian into Vanpur, or why.

The Lotus supposedly had two leaders, but the Vanpur cell dealt only with ‘one of their own’, a fellow Rajati. Every decision was made by [[Киран Варма|Kiran]], a Raksha hidden at the temple. Contact with him passed through a network of informers, and meetings were arranged sometimes within the city and sometimes beyond its walls.

Djevahar himself offered to arrange a conversation. He would report that a genuine customer had appeared and that the matter would brook no delay. He would not even need to lie.

Before the jeweller left, Urist reminded him of the requested brooch. His surname meant cactus, and Djevahar did indeed find a suitable piece—an ancient work from the Sunset Valley of [[Амон-Астат|Amon-Astat]]. Green inlays formed the silhouette of a little cactus, while a fine gold chain hung from the setting. Djevahar surrendered the brooch without payment, using it to bargain for the lives of himself, his wife and his son.

The son had already fled to Kiran. The travellers promised not to kill the boy—and had no intention of breaking their word.

Djevahar left to arrange the meeting. His wife returned to what remained of the stall, while the heroes stayed inside the house, drank masala tea and wore untroubled expressions. Any curious visitor was to be told that the master had gone to the warehouse. In truth, the woman remained an unspoken hostage while her husband hurried to do the bidding of those who had nearly burnt their shop. She already understood that nothing good could come of it and probably knew that Djevahar was accustomed to lying to more people than his enemies.

Gilja had still not decided Samir's fate. Kiran remained under the monastery's protection, no meeting had yet been appointed, and no one knew how to wake Chori's loved ones.

Yet the Black Lotus was no longer merely a symbol upon rings or a row of bodies in a dark alley. Its mercy now had a price, its needles an origin, and the man who made its decisions a name.

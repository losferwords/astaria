# Chapter 096 — Moon Hill

*[[Сурадж Ка Гхар|Suraj Ka Ghar]], 106 NE, autumn*

The guest cell at the House of Red Silence was stifling. Smoke drifted through the window, and water splashed in the courtyard: an attendant rinsed a bowl, poured the pink water into a gutter and reached for the next. The objects from the hiding place lay upon the table — four vials filled with blood, six empty ones, a yellowed manuscript, golden cups of the lesser measure and a black blade that scarcely reflected the daylight. The travellers had still not found [[Киран Варма|Kiran]], whom they intended to confront with their discoveries.

The monk waited at the threshold with his hands tucked into his sleeves. Two novices passed with a basket of laundry; a brief breath of cool air came from somewhere deep in the courtyard. The bound bells swayed beneath the cornice, the wind merely stirring the scarlet cloth around their tongues.

[[Урист Китин|Urist]] had no wish to hear further assurances that prayer was all that took place in the temple. He stepped towards the attendant while [[Томас Робинсон|Tom]] and [[Мэй Ву|Mei]] blocked the way out. [[Гилья Кангарлавехотри|Gilja]] translated the questions. Backed against a brick wall by a semicircle of armed foreigners, the monk raised his palms and begged them to refrain from violence in the house of [[Шубханкари|Shubhankari]]. This time, he told them more.

Kiran spent long hours amongst the sick, checking their condition and praying with the novices. He stopped particularly often beside those who had very little time left. Afterwards, these people disappeared from the infirmary. Their relatives continued to visit, yet the monk had never heard a complaint about the missing bodies. He believed that the instructor performed a special funerary rite for the dying, one the other attendants were not permitted to know about.

Twice, at the stream where he fetched water, the monk had seen Kiran with a grey-haired old man. The stranger wore no turban and, according to the attendant, did not look like a Rajati. They stood an arm's length apart in silence. The monk had not dared approach: a novice had no right to interfere in a Raksha's affairs.

The travellers ordered him to report immediately anything else he might notice. Having secured an informant, they left the temple towards evening. The paving was already being swept in the alley where the dead men of the [[Чёрный Лотос|Black Lotus]] had lain the day before. Upon the walls, the marks with which the heroes had tried to disguise the night's battle as a settling of scores between accomplices were still visible.

On the way to the Second Brew, the fur trader's son called out to Urist. The tiger cloak was ready, and the boy assured him that the work was of the finest quality. His mother, [[Савитри Эклунд|Savitri]], was still recovering under a Raksha's care, but both her son and her husband had already visited her. The boy led the guests to the shop.

[[Бату Эклунд|Batu]] greeted them with a directness that conveyed his gratitude more clearly than lengthy courtesies could have done. His wife had been found alive, his son no longer suffered the torment of uncertainty, and now he could at last deliver a sound piece of work to his customer. He spread the cloak upon the counter. The thick tiger fur was exactly what Urist had chosen when they first bargained.

Noticing the Kitin coat of arms, Batu studied his guest. Talk of surnames soon turned to kinship: Urist proved to be a second cousin of the craftsman [[Арно Эклунд|Arno Eklund]]. For Batu, that was enough to make his customer a kinsman. He shook Urist's hand firmly and produced a bottle of [[Авгарский самогон|Avgar moonshine]] from beneath the counter.

Gilja still needed to replenish his ritual spices. While the others settled in at Batu's, he went shopping in the market. The rich scent of herbs and spices rose above the stalls; red, yellow and almost black powders took their turns upon the scales. Gilja knew far more about the quality of seasonings than the art of bargaining down a price. His companions' advice did not save his purse: the mixture he needed cost him his entire bag of silver.

By the time he returned to the shop, the drinking had begun. Batu delighted in meeting a kinsman, Urist urged his friends to join them, and the bottle passed from hand to hand. Only Mei refused the moonshine and remained sober. The others abandoned the search for Kiran in their present state: returning to the temple with drunken threats, without even knowing where the instructor was, would have been far too foolish.

While the men were occupied with their cups, Mei drew from Gilja's belt the black blade found beneath the chest's false bottom. Taking the cups of the lesser measure as well, she went to find [[Велимир Сажин|Velimir]]. At least this part of the investigation could still continue.

At the Second Brew, Nadila stood behind the counter, her daughter served tea, and the [[Тхаги|Thuggee]] the travellers knew sat in the common room. Velimir was not amongst the customers. The landlady explained that he had rented a room with a red-haired northern woman. She had last seen them at a table, emptying glowing mushrooms from a bag and sorting them directly upon its surface.

The upstairs door was barred from within. Mei managed to lift the bar and entered. Velimir and [[Лиарин Ветреная|Liarin]] lay on neighbouring beds amongst scattered mushrooms. Both breathed steadily and slept soundly.

Mei began slapping the druid's face to wake her. A swarm of bees immediately rose from the bag beside the bed, but Mei did not retreat. At last, Liarin opened her eyes. Her movements were laboured, her gaze wandered across the ceiling, and she could not form her words. She nevertheless managed to say something in Nadair, and the bees returned to the bag. Velimir, whom Mei roused in the same fashion, struggled just as much to regain his senses. Both still needed several hours to recover fully.

Leaving them some water, Mei went downstairs. [[Чори Марджари|Chori]] had said that she had seen similar tiny golden cups at the Second Brew; it was time to discover why the teahouse kept them. In the kitchen, amongst the rice, vegetables and utensils beside the curry pot, Mei found two cups of the lesser measure. They had been polished to a shine and smelt of nothing; she could find no sign of recent use.

She stayed to watch. Nadila took ordinary cups, fetched food and spoke with her daughter. Then the landlady opened a cupboard, took out one of the golden cups and set it behind the counter without pouring anything into it.

Before long, a girl of about nineteen collected the cup. A hood almost concealed her face. She gave Nadila a brief nod and left, with Mei following behind.

The stranger moved swiftly through the alleys, avoiding the watch and choosing her way confidently amongst the lamplit streets. By nightfall, the pursuit had brought Mei back to the House of Red Silence.

Beside the stream, women carried basins of bloodstained linen. They were heading for the laundry — a small hut of bamboo and straw built directly above the running water. The stream flowed beneath the floor, with a little bridge spanning its open section. The patients' female relatives came here in their everyday clothes, so the hooded girl attracted no attention. She took a basin, joined the women and went inside. Mei did the same.

A single lamp burned in the laundry. Two women washed linen and spoke quietly of their husbands: the fever was growing worse, and the last few days had brought ever less hope. A statue of Shubhankari stood against the wall. The girl with the golden cup was nowhere to be seen.

Mei searched the little room but found no way the stranger could have left. The hut had a single entrance; the stream rushed beneath her feet, and a roof of bamboo and straw lay overhead. Mei had seen the girl enter, yet now she had vanished. Mei stayed amongst the washerwomen, began washing someone else's linen and continued to wait.

In the morning, Urist woke upon red silk in a canopied redwood bed. His hammer was with him, as was the tiger cloak; the rest of his clothing had disappeared. High ceilings, a carpet, paintings, stained-glass windows and candelabra made the room resemble royal chambers. Urist remembered the baths of Tanassar, though the furnishings here were richer still. Wine and fruit stood upon the table.

Unable to find his belongings, he wrapped himself in a sheet and went into the corridor. A Rajati servant carrying a tray started at the sight of him, then immediately straightened and addressed him respectfully. Urist discovered that he was in the palace of Vanpur's elder. The servant did not know how the guest had arrived, but the very room from which he had emerged demanded respectful treatment.

Urist tried to ask about Batu and his companions, describing an Avgar, a Rajati and a northerner. The servant could tell him nothing definite. The elder was already awake and eating breakfast; Urist was promised clothes and an enquiry into who had escorted him to his chambers. The Avgar remained to wait.

Gilja woke in a far humbler cell. An undressed Batu lay upon the neighbouring bed, and a bound girl sat between them. Spices were scattered across the floor before her — the very spices for which Gilja had emptied his purse. From the pattern, he understood that he had performed a Raksha rite during the night, but could not remember its purpose.

His own raktakataras were still with him. The black blade from the guest house was nowhere nearby. Gilja did not recognise the girl sitting on the floor; she watched him in terror and, unable to rise with her hands and feet bound, tried to inch towards Batu's bed.

All Gilja found in the cell was a bottle with a third of its moonshine left. There was no water, so he went into the corridor to look for some.

The underground chambers reminded him of the temple the travellers had once visited in the forest. Lamps burned beneath an earthen vault, the air was stale, and no daylight reached this place. Four cells opened off the corridor. Two images of Shubhankari stood to either side — one in the Cycle of Destruction, the other in the Cycle of Creation. Behind the statues, Gilja found only walls.

In the opposite cell lay a boy of about fourteen, sleeping in a fierce fever. Another held two unconscious elderly men with bandaged elbows; in a third lay an old woman and a woman of about thirty. Gilja found no water with any of them and stopped in the corridor.

Tom greeted the morning upon a mountain summit. [[Мерката|Mercate's]] black feathers lay all around him, and [[Мусака|Musaka]] stood nearby, watching the dawn. Tom was naked, but for the first time in many days he felt well: the pain and dull heaviness that had troubled him were gone. The morning chill touched his skin, jungle spread below, and Vanpur was visible some four kilometres away.

He could not reach the goddess. Tom remembered Mercate, [[Селина Эллиот|Selina]], the black needles, [[Город Награкшаса|Nagrakshasa]] and the task which, as far as he could recall, he had been carrying out alone. Urist, Mei and Gilja were absent from these memories. Nor could he explain how he had come to be upon the summit.

He found no belongings nearby apart from the feathers. Tom fashioned clothing from them and set off towards the city with Musaka.

The watch stopped him at the gates. Tom told them what remained in his memory: the previous day he had been drinking in the market with an Avgar who traded in furs, and now he was looking for his missing possessions. The watchmen knew Batu's name and let Tom through.

The trader's son was asleep beneath the shop counter. His father was nowhere to be found. Tom woke the boy roughly, misjudging his strength; the lad sprang up with several fresh bruises and at first could not understand what was wanted of him. He had tried moonshine for the first time in his life and remembered only the beginning of the drinking. He said there had been five of them, whereas Tom could recall only himself, Batu and Batu's son. Asked about the mountain, the boy said it was called Moon Hill.

Tom obtained a pair of the trader's work trousers, which barely reached his ankles. Looking around the kitchen, he noticed several objects that sat oddly in an ordinary fur shop: broad palace fans upon poles, of the sort carried in the elder's presence, and a basin containing blood and three teeth. Tom checked his own teeth, then the boy's. Neither had any missing.

Musaka picked up the scent of blood and led her master through the morning market to the Second Brew. Nadila greeted him from the doorway. The dog circled the common room, lingered beside the counter where the landlady received customers, and sat down.

At a table, Velimir and Liarin were eating curry. Tom recognised them without difficulty. The druid leapt up at the sight of his attire and asked where his armour had gone and how he had come by another man's trousers. Tom told her about the moonshine and waking upon the mountain.

Liarin recalled that Mei had visited them during the night. She had slapped them awake until the bees rushed to defend the druid. Liarin and Velimir themselves had not gone to the market: after taking the mushrooms, they had remained in their room and slept. Having heard her out, Tom asked who Mei was.

Nadila confirmed that she had seen Mei late in the evening, when she went upstairs to the northerners' room. The landlady could recall no visitor with a battered face or missing teeth. Musaka remained beside the counter; the trail of blood went no further.

# Calypso

## Main text

Calypso is the patron of [[Талассия|Thalassia]], goddess of luck, adventure, seafaring and invention. To the [[Талассийцы|Thalassians]], she embodies the fortunate chance offered not to an idle dreamer, but to one who has made a vessel ready and still dares to sail beyond the known chart.

Sailors portray Calypso as a young, blue-eyed woman in a strange metal garment resembling both armour and an intricate machine. No one alive can confidently attest to this likeness: the goddess has not appeared before her people for many years, and the old images differ markedly. Some place a ship's wheel in her hand; others show an unknown instrument or a gaming die.

The chronicles tell that Calypso encountered [[Хтониды|Chthonids]] exploring the western [[Полуостров Таллас|Tallas Peninsula]]. She taught the settlers to understand the sea, seek new paths and alter any device that could no longer meet its purpose. Under her guidance, the future Thalassians made shipbuilding and mechanics the foundations of their culture.

The worship of Calypso seldom demands strict temple ceremony. Before sailing, a captain might cast a coin into the harbour, dedicate a first successful design to the goddess or promise to name a newly discovered cape in her honour. Games of chance are also regarded as appeals to their patron, though her priests offer a warning: Calypso delights in courageous risk, not in those who ask her to bear responsibility for their own folly.

The goddess may choose the finest captains as [[Наварх|Navarchs]]. They treat every voyage as a chance to come nearer to the mystery of her disappearance. Thalassian expeditions therefore search not only for new harbours and trading routes, but for ancient traces of Calypso's presence.

Old accounts often name the goddess beside [[Хангор|Khangor]]. Calypso supplied the vision and the Smith-God gave it form—or, in another telling, they argued without end over which mattered more: inspiration or skill. Since both have long vanished from the daily lives of their peoples, these tales are now heard as parables of fortune joined with labour.

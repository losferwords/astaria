# Chapter 095 — House of Red Silence

*[[Сурадж Ка Гхар|Suraj Ka Ghar]], 106 NE, summer*

While [[Джевахар Ашма|Djevahar]] arranged a meeting with [[Киран Варма|Kiran]], the travellers waited in the house behind his jewellery shop. The heavy curtain separating the shop from the family's rooms still smelt of smoke after the recent fire, yet it could muffle neither the voices of the market nor the creak of the damaged counter. Djevahar's wife set flatbreads, lentil stew, salted vegetables and spiced tea before the guests. She maintained her composure, but tending to the food and customers merely helped her avoid thinking about where her husband had gone and who awaited his return.

Meanwhile, [[Урист Китин|Urist]] searched the house. Unobtrusive doors in the walls concealed tools, spare scales and papers, but one hollow proved to be a true hiding place. Inside lay an official order for roughly twenty tiny golden cups. The document specified the weight of each vessel, the composition of the metal, the stones and the design upon its sides. Dancing figures represented the unbroken passage between the cycles of Creation and Destruction, while a fine thread of garnets was to run between them.

No larger than walnuts, the vessels were not meant for an ordinary meal. Cups of this kind were used for household vows, temple offerings and the precise measuring of incense, medicines or sacrificial blood. Such a commission might have been unremarkable for a great temple in the capital, but seemed excessive for a single Raksha in Vanpur. The name at the bottom of the order was [[Киран Варма|Kiran Varma]].

Nearby, Urist found a redwood casket. Upon black silk lay a dagger whose blade was made of the same dark stone as [[Чори Марджари|Chori's]] weapon, with a bag of silver beside it. The hiding place also contained a lotus signet used as proof of the right to convene secret meetings. Urist took the order, the blade and the signet, but left the silver where it lay.

[[Мэй Ву|Mei]] waited until the counter grew quiet and questioned the jeweller's wife. The woman told her that Djevahar had become involved with dangerous people after their son disappeared. The boy had been the first to become entangled in their affairs; his father went looking for him and was caught in the same web. The lotus rings made by Djevahar had already drawn the watch's attention. More than once he had promised his wife that he would accept no further such commissions, and every time he had broken his word.

The woman asked for neither vengeance nor vindication. It was enough for her that her husband and son should remain alive. That simple hope affected Mei more deeply than she expected, reminding her of her own daughter.

Before long, the measured thump of spears and the copper chime of armour cut through the clamour of the market. Two watchmen entered the shop while another three blocked the way into the street. The Rajati wore long quilted coats reinforced with dark plates across the chest and shoulders, brick-red sashes of the city watch, round leather shields and short spears. A copper badge in the shape of the city gates and a red border upon his turban marked their senior officer.

Djevahar stood between the guards with his hands bound. Their leader set two objects upon the surviving part of the counter: a bloodstained black lotus ring and the tiny golden cup found in the jeweller's inner pocket. Djevahar watched the cup with greater anxiety than the evidence taken from one of the dead in the western alley.

When the watchmen saw [[Гилья Кангарлавехотри|Gilja]] bearing the marks of a Raksha and carrying his raktakataras, they instinctively drew themselves upright. They explained that Djevahar had been detained in connection with the bodies found in the alley and that they were now obliged to search the shop, the workshop and the living quarters. Ashma's hallmark stood upon the inside of the ring, and the watch wanted to know who had commissioned it.

Djevahar maintained that a craftsman's mark connected him to an object he had made, not to the crimes of its owner. The heroes tried to persuade the watch that the jeweller was merely an intermediary and that Kiran was the man they ought to seek. During the argument, however, Urist mentioned four bodies even though the watchmen had not yet given their number. Thomas attempted to draw attention away from the slip, but only deepened their suspicions.

The travellers were unable to prevent the arrest. Djevahar was taken to the guardhouse, and both the shop and workshop were sealed. The meeting he had meant to arrange had fallen through, although the most dangerous discoveries were already beyond the watch's reach.

By midday, the heroes were once more in the streets of Vanpur. Near the familiar laundry, Chori called down to them from a rooftop and threw Gilja a velvet pouch. It contained the golden cup confiscated from Djevahar. The girl had managed to steal it from one of his escorts.

Gilja now examined the vessel more closely. It was a cup of the lesser measure, a tiny ritual measure shaped like both a blossoming lotus and an oil lamp. Eight chased petals supported the rounded bowl, while the dancing figures and the garnet thread formed a closed circle of life. One petal, however, had deliberately been made shorter than the others. The order they had found contained no such detail, which meant that the finished cup was not only a piece of temple ware but also a secret sign.

Chori had already seen similar cups at the Second Brew and suggested seeking the trail there first. In her view, if they attacked the most conspicuous enemy at once, his accomplices would have time to scatter. Urist, however, wanted Kiran rather than another intermediary. The travellers set out for the temple, leaving Chori behind with her warning.

The temple rose above the close-packed rooftops of Vanpur in broad red terraces. Rain had darkened the old ochre upon its walls, and a golden cup crowned the stepped tower above the inner sanctuary. Hundreds of copper bells hung beneath the cornices, but the tongue of each had been bound with a strip of scarlet cloth. The wind moved them without a single note.

Gilja knew the reason for the silence. Vanpur lay close to [[Деревня Бадракали|Badrakali]], and the temple was known as the House of Red Silence. While the Cycle of Destruction continued, its bells were to remain mute as they awaited the next Creation.

A stairway worn by thousands of bare feet led to the main courtyard. The relatives of the sick brought bowls of rice, flowers and small silver coins. Attendants distributed water and bitter decoctions. Adults and children lay beneath the colonnades, many of them unconscious. The scents of sandalwood and cumin mingled with medicinal bitterness and the iron tang of blood. A dancing [[Шубханкари|Shubhankari]] had been carved above the entrance: one half of the goddess bore flowers and grain while the other held a blade and a ruby cup, yet both smiled alike. The temple courtyard increasingly resembled [[Самир Тхакур|Samir's]] overcrowded hospital.

Gilja entered the hall before the statue and demanded that Kiran be brought to him. A bald monk who had been counting grains of rice recoiled, fell to his knees and touched his forehead to the floor. He tried to begin with an ablution and communal prayer, but Gilja considered it dishonourable to pray in a house abandoned by its own Raksha. His raktakataras had not yet touched the monk, yet their power was already drawing the life from him.

The frightened attendant explained that one of the city's Rakshas, Samir, rarely left the hospital and kept a strict vow, while Kiran had instructed the monks to await his return. The monk either did not know where Kiran had gone or dared not say. At the travellers' demand, he led them to the house where the temple instructor stayed.

It was a small round cell intended for visiting Rakshas. Inside stood a cold hearth, a wooden bed, two windows on either side of a statue of Shubhankari, a circular rug and a table with a clean sheet of parchment. A vial of ochre stood beside it in place of ink. A beechwood chest contained a spare sash, cloth for a turban and writing quills.

Urist found a false bottom in the chest. The signet from Djevahar's house fitted the concealed lock so precisely that it seemed to have been made for it. Beneath lay a locked box, two golden cups with shortened petals, a large blade of black stone and a yellowed manuscript. The dark shard was already close to the size of a raktakatara, and Gilja considered its very presence in a Raksha's dwelling an act of blasphemy.

Most of the manuscript was written in such archaic Rajati that Gilja understood only a few verbs and prepositions. Upon the first page, however, the name of [[Хангор|Hangor]] had been written in Avgari script. The rest resembled the description of an unknown craft or ancient invention, but none of them could interpret it with confidence.

Mei picked the lock of the box. Ten vials stood in wooden compartments, four of them filled with blood. None bore a label. Gilja determined that it was not Shubhankari's blood, while combining samples revealed that the contents came from four different people.

In the hands of a Raksha, another person's blood could become a means of acting upon its owner. Gilja therefore suspected that Kiran kept the blood of influential people in Vanpur and used it to subject the Black Lotus to his will. Another possibility remained: perhaps the temple instructor himself had created the organisation to gather money and blood for the search for a cure. All the heroes knew for certain was that the Lotus's people more often cast their victims into unwaking sleep than killed them. Neither theory yet explained everything they had found.

The monk claimed that Kiran had left the previous day and that such absences occurred roughly three times a week. The temple instructor conducted prayers and rites beyond the temple, but the attendants were not entitled to know where he lived between visits. The monk knew nothing of the black blade and reminded them that other Rakshas had occupied the guest house before Kiran. It was possible that Kiran did not even know of the compartment beneath the chest.

The temple kept no list of former guests. The attendant sent someone to the city's ruler to learn the names of the Rakshas who had stayed at the House of Red Silence before Kiran. Mei followed the messenger and made certain that he truly ran to carry out the errand rather than going to warn accomplices.

Of Kiran himself, the monk could add only that the temple instructor had seemed sombre and despondent for the past month. Roughly the same amount of time had passed since the travellers emerged from the marshes and encountered a naga on the approach to the city. The connection might have been mere coincidence: the attendants denied having any dealings with nagas and insisted that they concerned themselves only with prayer and aiding those who suffered. Meanwhile, the number of sick people in the temple continued to grow.

Before leaving, Urist examined the black blade as a craftsman. He suspected that blades of this kind were not forged but produced by carefully striking pieces from a large block. By his estimate, the shard they had found might yield roughly four small blades like the black needles, with many smaller fragments left over. They decided to show the stone to [[Велимир Сажин|Velimir]].
